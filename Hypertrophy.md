CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
 
 
When I was 21 years old, my life was dedicated to college baseball and I had
 
high hopes of playing professionally.
 
 
 
But, one game I threw a slider and heard a loud pop in my elbow followed by
 
a sharp stabbing pain. I was devastated to learn that I had blown out some of
 
the tendons in my elbow. I thought that my baseball dreams were over. I had
 
reconstructive surgery and then had my arm in a mechanical brace that would
 
slowly straighten it out by 10 degrees each week over a month and a half. I
 
remember the first time I got my arm out of the brace; it looked like a wet
 
noodle. It was completely emaciated.
 
 
 
I was able to start throwing a ball at three months post-op. I went out to play
 
catch with a tennis ball with my mother. I was unable to throw it 25 feet.
 
That moment was a turning point for me. I was so focused on what I couldn’t
 
do, that I had not yet explored the potential in what I could do. I vividly
 
remember saying to my mom “F-this!! If my arm is going to be sh&t, then I am
 
going to make the rest of my body as strong as possible. I will become the
 
strongest athlete on the team or in the league! I’ll find a way to make up for
 
this.”
 
 
Up until this point, I was not into training beyond what was required for the
 
baseball team. In fact, I definitely babied myself using excuses like “I don’t
 
want to hurt my arm, knees, or rotator cuff,” or “I don’t want to lose
 
flexibility,” or “I’ll be too tired to play on the field.” Being gifted enough to be
 
pretty strong at 6 feet tall and 185 pounds (lb) without really investing in
 
training had gotten me pretty far. But now, after elbow surgery, things were
 
different. That special gift that had got me to Division 1 baseball was gone
 
and all I had was a desire to find a way to continue.
 
 
I was starting from scratch in terms of training after this injury. My physical
 
therapist was a great guy named Mickey. He would take me through some
 
shoulder and elbow rehabilitation exercises and then we would lift. I couldn’t
 
do much at first and the program was very basic. It was three sets of 10
 
repetitions of the following exercises:
 
●
Leg Press
 
●
Leg Extension
 
●
Hamstring Curl

I did this routine three times a week for three months.
 
 
 
There is something really beautiful starting from scratch. You’re just a blank

attitude and outlook as well. I had played sports for a while up until this
 
point, but I was never really hungry. Hungry with a sense of desperation that
 
is. This period of training was the fondest in my career. Don’t get me wrong, I
 
sucked and had a really long way to go, but there was a feeling of insatiable
 
desire to train and be one percent better each day.
 
 
 
This focus on daily improvement started bleeding into my nutrition, sleep,
 
habits, and mindset. I started reading training books and magazines sifting
 
through the basics. Eat more protein; check. Cut out added sugar; check. Get
 
eight hours of sleep; check.
 
 
 
By the end of March, I was maxing out all the machines down to the last pin
 
and I had gained 10 pounds. Seeing those results just drove my hunger to train
 
even more. However, my routine had become stale. I wasn’t seeing the same
 
results as fast anymore. I had an acquaintance at school named Adam. Adam
 
was a friendly guy from my hometown who competed in bodybuilding. I told
 
him what I was doing and that I needed some help. He said to show up at 5
 
AM at a gym in town the next morning.
 
 
 
That first morning was EARLY. Five AM is not conducive to the college lifestyle
 
and this was extracurricular training for me. The baseball team would get in
 
the weight room twice a week for about 45 minutes and I knew that if I
 
wanted to be the strongest player in the league, it would take more. Adam
 
laid out the routine and the first thing I noticed was some new lifts. We lifted
 
twice a week alternating between these two sessions (sets x reps):

Then, we would change the exercises. Having the new rep schemes, loading,
 
and movements kept my progress going. Adam also knew a lot about nutrition
 
which for me basically boiled down to eating more food to drive muscle
 
growth.
 
 
I carried this routine through the summer, much to the disagreement of my
 
baseball coaches and teammates who kept saying “You’re gonna get too big!
 
Are you a baseball player or a professional wrestler?” But there was one
 
comparison I just couldn’t get over: 
​
if I wanted to play at the next level don’t I
 
need to look like athletes at the next level?
​
 Major League All-Stars look like
 
Greek gods; their physical stature is visibly different from the collegiate level.
 
 
 
So, by the next season, I was up to 220 pounds and cut. I had gained nearly
 
30 pounds of muscle over a year (2-3 lb/month) simply by training hard and
 
consistently combined with eating more calories. Players and coaches on
 
different teams didn’t recognize me. I had gone up a shirt size and added four
 
inches to my waist. My bench had increased from 225 to 315 pounds and my
 
squat had increased from 315 to 500 pounds. But the most important change
 
was I could throw, and throw harder than before.
 
 
For all the flack I took from the baseball team about bodybuilding, it was
 
actually my new stature that got me a shot in professional baseball years
 
later. It was this willingness to forgo conventional training norms and
 
embrace an unorthodox approach that ultimately got me to the competitive
 
level I desired. This also developed a passion for and a career in training,
 
healthy living, and sharing information with fellow athletes. It’s all about
 
applying a methodology that best suits one's aims.

Muscle Tension
 
Muscle tension is the force generated by the muscle on an object such as an
 
external weight. This force is a result of a contraction that can be one of
 
three types:
 
 
●
isometric (the muscle length doesn’t change in a static hold),
 
●
concentric (the muscle length shortens like in a bicep curl), and
 
 
●
eccentric (muscle length lengthens like in the lowering phase of a
 
squat; the contraction is effectively a “brake” mechanisms).
 
 
 
How much force a muscle can generate is in part dictated by the number of
 
actin and myosin myofilaments present in the muscle fibers. A greater
 
number of filaments means there is a greater ceiling (potential maximum) of
 
force generation. When a person increases the load they are trying to resist or
 
lift, one adaptation response is to synthesize more actin or myosin. This
 
increase in the number of filaments is one of the reasons the cells grow in
 
size. The associated cellular machinery to make these protein filaments (e.g.,
 
endoplasmic reticulum) also grows to match production demand.
 
 
 
More filaments aren’t the only thing that is needed to produce more force,
 
however. Metabolic stress (discussed below) is closely related because energy
 
production from metabolism is needed to create muscle tension in certain
 
applications.

Neurological Adaptations
 
While these histological changes are occurring, neurological adaptations also
 
occur to make a stronger athlete. For example, training increases motor unit
 
recruitment (a motor unit is a neuron and the muscle fibers it innervates) and
 
muscle synchronization. This is a neurological adaptation where simply
 
refining one’s movement by repetition helps the brain innervate more muscle
 
fibers while simultaneously making the body work together more efficiently.
 
This explains why no-load or low-intensity practice can help make someone
 
stronger.
 
 
 
This is a feed-forward mechanism contributing to hypertrophy, where the
 
neurological changes don’t directly increase muscle size but they do allow an
 
athlete to lift more or perform work faster at subsequent training sessions.
 
This ultimately increases overall hypertrophy.

Metabolic Stress in Functional Fitness
 
Metabolic stress correlates with muscle tension. The energy needed to allow
 
the muscles to produce muscle tension comes from metabolism. The more
 
energy needed, particularly when moving as fast as possible, the more
 
metabolic stress will occur. This is in terms of metabolic waste by-products
 
as well as simply the stress to produce more energy.
 
 
 
When workouts are performed with intensity, this pushes one to the limit of
 
the body’s capacity to produce energy (which drives the body to adapt to a
 
higher level). However, playing with rest periods in a more classic weight

rep sets of bench press at 185 pounds. Instead of resting 3-5 minutes
 
between the sets, you rest for 2 minutes. You will not get as many reps, but
 
there is still a benefit via metabolic stress. Or even 60 seconds on, 60
 
seconds off (rest) for max reps has the potential for a metabolic adaptation.

variable work: rest intervals to complete the work in a short period is great
 
for creating metabolic stress.

duration) there is a reduced effect in the metabolic stress as energy is
 
produced from a different pathway. There is still muscle tension and strength
 
benefits there, but less so from a metabolic stress perspective.

Setting the Foundations for Upper Body Pressing and Pulling
 
 
With everything in training, proper foundations are the key to athletic success
 
and longevity in training. Here are some notes on creating foundational
 
positions for upper body pressing and pulling movements.
 
 
 
Pressing Movements
 
●
In dips, establish a support and press with the elbows in tight to the
 
body.
 
●
In a bench or strict press, externally rotate the elbow pits forward so
 
the elbows do not flare out.
 
●
Press the shoulder in a direction opposite to the load. For example, in a
 
dip push the shoulders down, in a push up and bench press push the
 
shoulders away from the body, and in the handstand push and press
 
drive the shoulders up to the ears. This gives the most muscular
 
support to the shoulders.
 
 
Pull Ups and Hanging Movements
 
 
●
Establish a grip slightly outside the shoulders so there is an optimal
 
range of motion and muscular engagement. Too wide of a grip sacrifices
 
strength and too narrow of a grip restricts the range of motion.
 
●
Use a full grip that gets the knuckles on top of the bar. This shortens
 
the lever arm as well as provides a stronger grip.
 
●
Pull the shoulders down slightly to engage the lats and put the
 
shoulders in a neutral position. Yielding to your body weight in the hang
 
puts most of the tension on the AC joint of the shoulder.
 
●
Have the capacity to do at least one strict pull up before you start
 
kipping. The negative eccentric loading of the kip is often beyond the
 
capacity of the shoulder if you don’t have a strict single pull up.

CHAPTER 5: WHEN HYPERTROPHY IS AT ODDS WITH FUNCTIONAL FITNESS
 
 
While there can be a synergistic effect of hypertrophy training with functional
 
fitness, training can become skewed too much towards hypertrophy such that
 
there is a negative effect on one’s fitness. This is not problematic if the
 
athlete wants that outcome. However, it is important to realize that
 
hypertrophy training is with the goal to increase size and at some point, that
 
size may limit certain applications.
 
 
For example, there is a fine line in terms of bodyweight for the optimal weight
 
at which someone can perform a high number of consecutive pull-ups as well
 
as consecutive double bodyweight back squats. Increasing one’s leg strength
 
with hypertrophy training will surely increase their back squat capacity, but
 
the result may be a loss in pull-up volume because the upper body cannot lift
 
the heavier legs for as many repetitions. This is why one’s top-end
 
hypertrophy is limited on a functional fitness program like CrossFit®. There is
 
enough long duration running, box jumps, pull-ups, etc., that the body will not
 
put on 
​
excessive
​
 amounts of muscle. Some sport-specific athletes are willing
 
to take this trade-off. A football player may not need 50 consecutive pull-ups
 
as much as they need leg strength to sprint (relatively) short distances and
 
tackle other athletes. So the training methodology must align to the athlete’s
 
goals and desired applications for training.

Your priority should be taking the time to build a strong foundation! Here are

1) Develop Proper Core Strength
 
Back injuries are the number one reason lifters stop lifting! Bulging, herniated,

the hips, knees, and shoulders. A neutral spine position evenly loads the discs
 
of the spine reducing shear and creating a safe distribution of forces. Learn
 
how to create and hold this position before lifting any substantial weight. It
 
will also pay off in the long-term as the core will serve as the transmission of
 
force for moving big weight in back squats, cleans, deadlifts, etc. The best
 
athletes have a powerful and resilient core.

After the core, it’s all about strengthening the big movers: the hips, knees,
 
and shoulders. This also means strengthening the muscular surrounding them:
 
the glutes, hip flexors, adductors and abductors for the hips; hamstrings,
 
quadriceps, and calves for the knees; and the lats, pecs, and deltoids for the
